# Review: blind spots in the entries and in the hill-climbing machine (2026-10-10)

Chris asked for an all-up review after three runs: what we are missing in the algorithms, what
we are missing in the harness, and how the machine gets better at improving itself.

## 1. The machine's record so far

48 experiments, 18 kept (11 on their own, seven more on Chris's decisions), 23 rejected, the
rest flat, measurements or not run. 2,577 Spot node-minutes in all, about NZ$11 by the runbook formula.

Last night's run is the clearest lesson. Eleven experiments ran and one won. Most predictions
missed, and they missed in one direction, too high:

|---|---|---|---|
| hc-030 scratch shrink | +3–8% (reviews), +0–3% (mine) | +1.3% | mine right, reviews wrong |
| hc-031 dense to 191 | +2–5% (mine), +5–13% (Grok) | −7.2% | wrong sign |
| hc-032 sparse immediates | 0–3% | +0.6% | right |

Three of eleven landed in their predicted range. The cause is plain: the run skipped the
brief's first rule, "profile before guessing" (`AUTOPILOT.md` section 3). It took the two
reviews' ideas, and their cycle estimates, without a fresh phase profile. Neither review had
counters either; both said so. Ideas from reviews are cheap and welcome, but each one has to
name a phase and the share of cycles at stake before it gets node time.

## 2. Blind spots in the entries

**The wheel's real rival is danielspaangberg, and we have never measured against him.** Every
wheel run used rogiervandam's C5 as its control, but C5 is tagged `algorithm=other`. The top
faithful wheel entries are danielspaangberg's `PrimeC/solution_2` variants
(`5760of30030_owrb`, `480of2310_owrb`). On the Threadripper ours runs at about twice his speed,
from the leaderboard; on the Raspberry Pi 4 his mod-30030 wheel is the fastest faithful entry
of all at 1T. We don't know where ours lands there.

**Three of the five official runners have never run our code.** Every decision rests on Zen 3,
Zen 4, Zen 5 and Cobalt 100. The runners are a Zen 5 Threadripper, an AVX2 EPYC VM, an Intel
Core i7-9750H (Coffee Lake, AVX2), a Celeron 3865U (SSE4.2) and a Raspberry Pi 4
(Cortex-A72). For the Intel and Celeron we have only the sandbox Xeon with a forced target,
which is noisy and a different core. For the Pi we have Cobalt 100 (Neoverse N2), which is
three generations newer, four times wider and has four times the cache. NEON gang width and
the arm64 dense path were tuned on N2 only.

**The Zig wheel is 12–18% faster with the same design, and we can't say why.** After hc-033
and hc-035 the group loop, the sparse loop and the allocation look alike in the assembly. The
difference is somewhere we haven't looked: the candidate scan, pattern building, the tile and
13 phases, or code layout. That is priority 1 of the next run.

**The wheel's sparse phase, about half the pass, has had little work.** hc-003, hc-015 and
hc-018 touched the base's sparse loop; for the wheel only hc-012 (two primes a loop, lost at
16T) was tried. The byte kernel lost 9% on the Xeon sandbox but has never run on Zen 5, which
has two store ports.

**Shapes never prototyped:** a mod-210 wheel (48 planes; `RESEARCH.md` argues the planes get
too short for 16-word gangs, but that is an argument, not a measurement), and anything
specific to a narrow in-order-ish core like the A72.

**Thread scaling.** We measure 16 vCPUs. The Threadripper runs 192 threads; all-threads gains
like hc-035's (+2% to +3.4%, from fewer load-port slots under SMT) may grow or vanish there.

## 3. Blind spots in the harness

1. **No phase profile in the loop.** Azure exposes no PMU counters; the sandbox has no `perf`
   for its kernel. The hc-001 profile used TSC timers, by hand, once. It should be a standing
   diagnostic build, run at the start of every run on Zen 3 and Zen 5, for ours and the rival.
2. **Wrong wheel rival** (section 2). The harness builds C5, Rust, davepl and our entries; it
   should build danielspaangberg's best wheel variant too.
3. **Spot preemption loses whole tasks.** Both Zen 5 nodes went at 16:00 UTC; the 10-round
   confirmation survived only because I salvaged the streamed log by hand.
   `tools/salvage.py` now does that.
4. **Slow decisions.** One decision takes 30 to 40 minutes: a node's 8-minute start task, then
   six rounds of five builds at four thread counts. Most candidates lose by more than 2% on
   the first two rounds. A screen of three rounds at 1T and all threads on Zen 5, then the full
   protocol only for survivors, would roughly double the experiments per hour.
5. **One candidate per task.** Each task runs the champion twice per round as an A/A check.
   Two or three candidates against one champion in the same task would share that cost.
6. **Static screening by hand.** I wrote a loop analyser three times last night;
   `tools/loopstat.py` keeps it. Its limits are known: hc-035 cut 24 instructions a step and
   gained 0.5% at 1T. Use it to reject (as with hc-034), not to predict.
7. **Stale state.** Seven merged experiments still read "queued" on the dashboard until today.
   Merges done on Chris's decisions need the same status update as autopilot merges.
8. **Coverage of the official runners** (section 2): no Intel AVX2 machine, no SSE4-only
   machine, no A72.

## 4. How the machine gets better

The fix for most of section 3 is a habit, not a tool: close every run with a short
retrospective, and start the next run with the top harness change.

**Retrospective, at the end of every run** (added to `AUTOPILOT.md` section 9), in
`results/hc/RETRO.md`:
- the prediction table above: predicted range, measured result, in range or not;
- cost per kept change (node-minutes) and node-minutes lost to idle floors and preemption;
- which protocol steps were skipped, and why;
- at most three harness changes, added to `HARNESS-BACKLOG.md` in rank order.

**Harness changes first.** Each run spends its first 30 to 40 minutes on the top item of
`HARNESS-BACKLOG.md`, with Azure available to test it. The current top three: the standing
phase profile (with the Zig wheel), danielspaangberg as a wheel rival, and the two-stage
screen.

**Calibration as a metric.** The share of predictions that land in range is the machine's own
score. It was 3 of 11 last night. A run that skips the profile will show it there.
